Chipotle

01US fast casual

What they do best

Turned cups and bags into reading material: its “Cultivating Thought” series printed two-minute essays on packaging across 1,600 restaurants. In-store QR scans earn Rewards points.

Lesson: it drew backlash for featuring no Mexican authors. The words on the bag have to come from the culture.

Steal this

Make the bag worth reading: a dish story or a spice fact, so it becomes a reason to keep it.

Sourced Fast Company · Hospitality Tech · Fox

Packaging

Panda Express

02US quick service

What they do best

A single storytelling illustration on the takeout box (landmarks framed through an archway) nods to the classic American takeout box. It also moved from styrofoam to paperboard.

Steal this

One illustration on the box that sums up the food and the place it comes from.

Sourced QSR · NRN

Cava

03US fast casual

What they do best

Custom dishware that is bright white and contoured inside to flatter the food, with textured geometry outside. Its bold all-caps lettering is based on Levantine sign painting.

Steal this

Keep the inside of the bowl plain so the food is the colour, and put the pattern on the outside.

Sourced Polygraph · Slant

Sweetgreen

04US fast casual

What they do best

Collins’s refresh (custom type, a hand-drawn vintage-cookbook feel) runs across the store, signage, uniforms, packaging and the app as one system.

Lesson: its early loyalty (about $9 back per $99 spent) felt too slow. The first reward has to come fast.

Steal this

Run one system across the apron, the bag and the app so the brand feels inevitable.

Sourced NRN · It’s Nice That · NoGood

What they do best

Pentagram’s flexible kit adapts to street sites, airports and stadiums, while local artists paint murals at individual Shacks that mix neighbourhood landmarks into the brand.

Keep the brand kit fixed and let a local artist paint one wall.

Since 1995, hand-drawn cows holding misspelled signs have been the face of the brand, later spilling onto cow-print packaging and plush toys.

The 9 principles they all repeat

Strip away the logos and the big chains run the same nine plays. Tap one to see it in plain words.

01Design system over one-offs

The big brands don’t just buy a logo. They buy a kit of colours, type, icons and patterns, then use it on everything.

Who does it: Pentagram, Collins and Lippincott deliver kits, not logos. Sweetgreen rolled one system across store, uniforms, packaging and app.

For Curry District: One kit: wordmark, two or three colours, the spice-scale icons and one pattern. After that, every bag, menu and post is quick to put together. See the brand kit

02Own one colour, or one pattern

Repeat the same colour or pattern until people recognise you before they can read your name.

Who does it: Cava owns a sunny yellow, Wingstop green-and-gold, In-N-Out red-and-yellow, Taco Bell purple, and Five Guys a checkerboard.

For Curry District: Pick a pair and stick to it: marigold + rani pink (Bazaar) or saffron-gold on aubergine (Royal). Compare the palettes

03A character or a hand-drawn voice

A face, a doodle or a handwriting style gives a big brand the warmth of a corner shop.

Who does it: Chick-fil-A’s hand-drawn cows with their misspelled signs, and Chipotle’s chunky hand-drawn lettering and doodles.

For Curry District: A friendly steam-curl or chai-cup character (never a deity) for stickers and posts, plus a hand-lettered line or two. See the social kit

04Packaging as a billboard

The bag and the box get seen in kitchens, offices and photos long after the order. Treat them like ad space.

Who does it: Chipotle printed essays on cups and bags, In-N-Out hides prints on wrappers, and Panda Express tells a story on its box.

For Curry District: Every takeout and Uber Eats bag that leaves the kitchen is a free ad on someone’s counter, and in their first photo. See the packaging

05Sensory brand moments

A sound, a smell or a small gesture people remember, and tell their friends about.

Who does it: Taco Bell based its packaging on the sound of its bell, Five Guys leaves free peanuts at the door, and Cane’s hangs disco balls.

For Curry District: The tadka sizzle, cardamom chai you can smell at the door, a free papad while you wait. Small rituals people talk about. See the room ideas

06A QR code that leads to loyalty

Put a scan-me code where people already are (the bag, the table) and make the first reward arrive quickly.

Who does it: Chipotle customers scan an in-store QR to earn points. Sweetgreen learned that about $9 back per $99 spent felt too slow.

For Curry District: A QR on every bag and table that opens a stamp card, with the first treat coming quickly. We’d check what your Clover ordering can support first. See loyalty ideas

07Limited drops and merch

Something that is only here for a short time gives people a reason to come back this week, not “sometime”.

Who does it: Starbucks’ holiday cup day, In-N-Out’s yearly tee, Taco Bell’s rotating specials and Chipotle’s merch rewards.

For Curry District: One drop day a season, like a festival sweets box or a monsoon-chai cup, that regulars put in their calendar. See the festival calendar

08Local-artist murals

One wall painted by someone from the neighbourhood makes a chain location feel local and gives people a photo spot.

Who does it: Shake Shack and Raising Cane’s both use local artists so that each location is a one-off.

For Curry District: A DFW artist paints one wall (marigolds, jali, a nod to Little Elm), and it becomes the photo everyone posts. See the wall concepts

09Seasonal packaging

Changing one small thing each season makes a familiar brand feel new without a redesign.

Who does it: Starbucks has reset its holiday cup every year since 1997.

For Curry District: Keep the same plain cup and swap in a new sticker or sleeve each season. It’s the cheapest kind of news there is. See the seasons

04

Do this. Never that.

What the best Indian restaurants get right, and the clichés that make a place look like every other curry house.

Do

  • Pick one specific story and one hero item, and make both ownable.
  • Commission or draw real, hand-lettered signage.
  • Use pattern as a system (icons, borders, packaging), not as wallpaper.
  • Design packaging for the delivery-bag glance: one colour on a dark box (Royal) or a sticker-bold box (Bazaar).
  • Use puns and Hinglish lightly, and get them right.
  • Let the food, not the decor, supply most of the colour in the premium direction.

Don’t

  • No elephants, turbans, deity or sacred imagery.
  • No tri-colour flag palette.
  • No mandala wallpaper, Papyrus-style faux-Indian fonts or brown-and-beige.
  • Don’t copy another brand’s marks or artwork. Learn from how they work instead.
  • Don’t claim awards, reviews or dietary labels for Curry District that haven’t been verified.

Swap the cliché for something better

Every lazy shortcut has a better move that costs about the same.

  • Skip

    Elephant & regal-figure murals

    Even well-made ones (Gymkhana Dallas) are what everyone else does.

    Instead

    A personal story object. Chai Pani’s wooden tigers came from an ad in the owner’s hometown. A specific story beats a generic animal.

  • Skip

    Saffron-white-green flag colours

    Reads as a national flag, not a restaurant.

    Instead

    A colour pair that’s yours. Marigold + rani pink for Bazaar, or saffron-gold on aubergine for Royal.

  • SkipeXotic

    “Exotic” faux-Devanagari fonts

    Papyrus-style lettering and “mystical India” copy.

    Instead

    Real lettering. Dishoom hired an Indian-typeface expert, and Chai Pani had artists hand-letter its signs.

  • Skip

    Brown-and-beige “curry house”

    The palette that makes great food look tired.

    Instead

    Let the food bring the colour. Use a jewel-toned or cream background. Indienne went pastel pink and green instead of maroon-and-gold.

  • Skip

    Mandala wallpaper everywhere

    Pattern on every surface reads as noise.

    Instead

    Pattern as a system. Use it on borders, stickers, packaging and one feature wall, the way Curry Up Now and Pondicheri do.

  • Skip

    Deities, sacred symbols, Taj Mahal silhouettes

    Sacred imagery isn’t decoration.

    Instead

    Celebrate craft instead: block-print, jali, marigolds, truck-art lettering and chai culture.

05

What this means for Curry District

You don’t need a chain’s budget, just its discipline. Here’s where we’d start.

  1. Start with the bag

    Ideas 1–3 are all in the cheapest tier, and they go home with every takeout and Uber Eats order. It’s the fastest way to look like a chain without becoming one.

  2. One story, one hero dish

    Dishoom picked a Bombay café. Behrouz picked royal biryani. Curry District’s story is already in the name, the district, and the long biryani list is a natural place to crown a hero.

  3. One wall worth a photo

    If there’s budget for one bigger move, we’d pick a local-artist wall. Cane’s, Shake Shack and Chai Pani all made theirs the photo moment.
